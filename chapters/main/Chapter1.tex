\section{Background}
The Internet of Things (IoT) connects physical objects, sensors, and various devices through communication networks, enabling data-driven services in domains such as healthcare, industrial automation, and smart environments. Alongside the expansion of IoT deployments, the number of connected components exposed to cybersecurity threats increases correspondingly~\cite{atzori2010}. The Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) protocol is commonly used for lightweight publish-subscribe communication in IoT environments. However, MQTT-based systems can become targets for denial-of-service (DoS) and distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks. The DoS/DDoS-MQTT-IoT dataset, collected from a physical IoT testbed, provides normal MQTT traffic alongside ten DoS attack scenarios to facilitate the study of attacks in this context~\cite{alatram2023}. Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) monitor network activity to identify traffic or behavior exhibiting malicious signs. Machine learning and deep learning methods have been utilized and researched for this task due to their ability to learn characteristic patterns directly from network traffic. Their effectiveness depends partly on the availability and representativeness of the training data~\cite{he2009}. Federated Learning (FL) allows multiple clients to collaboratively train a shared model while keeping their data local; a central server distributes the global model and aggregates updates from clients across training rounds~\cite{mcmahan2017}. This approach is highly suitable when IoT data is distributed across multiple devices or locations, although communication and computational costs remain critical factors to consider in resource-constrained environments~\cite{shi2016, imteaj2022}. Keeping data local also does not equate to completely eliminating privacy concerns arising from the exchange of model updates~\cite{zhu2019}.

Privacy-preserving synthetic data generation is a potential solution to address the shortage of training data for rare attack classes in IoT networks while still ensuring strict security guarantees. Rather than relying on deep neural networks that require substantial computational resources and face convergence difficulties on edge devices~\cite{behera2022fedsyn}, the probabilistic graphical model paradigm (Probabilistic Graphical Models) approaches the problem by decomposing tabular data into low-dimensional marginal frequency distribution slices (low-dimensional marginals)~\cite{zhang2017privbayes}. Through the Chow-Liu dependency tree structure (Chow-Liu Tree)~\cite{chow1968}, this approach effectively captures the core correlations between traffic features and attack labels without being affected by the curse of dimensionality. When combined with Differential Privacy (DP)~\cite{zhang2017privbayes} on contingency tables and consistent graphical inference mechanisms (such as Private-PGM), the system can reconstruct a valid global distribution to generate conditional traffic data, establishing a foundation to enhance the performance of downstream intrusion detection systems (IDS)~\cite{mckenna2019pgm, mckenna2024aim}.
\section{Problem Statement}
Building an effective IDS for IoT networks faces multiple challenges related to data privacy, the representation of attack classes, and the practical costs of the model training process. These challenges are particularly evident when training data is distributed among clients and synthetic attack samples are considered as a supplementary detection solution. The first problem relates to privacy risks during federated training. Although FL avoids the periodic transfer of raw local data, clients must still exchange model updates with the server. Previous studies have demonstrated that shared gradients can leak information about training data in certain attack scenarios~\cite{zhu2019}. Therefore, sharing model weights instead of raw data cannot be considered a comprehensive privacy protection measure. Depending on the threat model and the type of information to be protected, supplementary mechanisms may be mandatory. The second problem concerns the representation of attack classes in the training data. Class imbalance can bias the learning process towards majority classes, making it difficult to identify classes that account for a low percentage~\cite{he2009}. For the DoS/DDoS detection problem in MQTT, the impact of this issue depends on the dataset version, label mapping, preprocessing pipeline, and data partitioning strategy for clients. Therefore, the class distribution of the dataset after selection and preprocessing must be empirically verified rather than assumed by default. If some attack classes lack data, synthetic samples can supplement training examples. However, merely increasing the number of samples does not prove that the generated data is useful; their actual contribution must be evaluated through data quality metrics and the detection performance of the downstream IDS model.

The third challenge pertains to the hardware constraints of edge devices, the ``curse of dimensionality,'' and the intricate trade-off among privacy protection, data quality, and operational overhead: on the one hand, deploying traditional distributed deep generative models (such as GANs or CVAEs) requires substantial computational resources (GPU/CPU) and large RAM capacity at client nodes, which is infeasible for hardware-constrained edge IoT devices~\cite{behera2022fedsyn}. On the other hand, tabular MQTT network traffic inherently possesses a high-dimensional structure with numerous interdependent attributes. Applying Differential Privacy (DP) directly to this high-dimensional attribute space readily leads to a state-space explosion---reaching up to $10^{19}$ cells~\cite{mckenna2019pgm}---causing the injected differential privacy noise to completely overwhelm the original data signal and severely degrade the fidelity of the generated distributions~\cite{zhang2017privbayes}.

A direct consequence is a severe tug-of-war among three factors: the degree of privacy protection (privacy budget $\varepsilon$), synthetic data utility for downstream IDS models, and computational/operational overhead across the IoT infrastructure. Specifically, tightening privacy guarantees (reducing the budget $\varepsilon$) injects heavy DP noise that destroys the core feature correlations of rare DoS/DDoS attack classes~\cite{zhang2017privbayes, mckenna2024aim}. Conversely, increasing the privacy budget and expanding model capacity to improve the quality of generated data causes computational costs and system runtime to surge drastically~\cite{mckenna2024aim}. Consequently, finding a tabular data generation solution that simultaneously ensures rigorous privacy guarantees, remains computationally lightweight for edge devices, and preserves critical feature characteristics for rare attack classes remains a major, unresolved technical bottleneck.

\section{Research Design and Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Research Type and Experimental Control}\label{subsec:research_type}
This study is a quantitative, controlled experimental study carried out on one published network-traffic dataset, DoS/DDoS-MQTT-IoT~\cite{alatram2023}. In the terminology of experimental research methodology, it combines controlled experiments with archival research: the independent variables (generative architecture, privacy protection mechanism, and differential privacy budget) are systematically manipulated, while the underlying network traffic comprises recorded real-world traces rather than synthetic traffic generated de novo for this study.

\subsubsection{Research Object}
The object of study is a distributed privacy-preserving data synthesis framework, PP-FedData, which enables multiple IoT/MQTT gateways to collaboratively construct synthetic network telemetry without releasing raw packets. Consequently, downstream Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) can be trained on representative traffic distributions that no single edge gateway could pool independently. PP-FedData is evaluated across four primary dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Downstream Utility:} The detection accuracy of the synthetic data for downstream classifiers;
    \item \textbf{Formal Privacy Guarantees:} The mathematical bounds and empirical leakage resistance of released artefacts;
    \item \textbf{System Overhead:} Communication bandwidth (uplink/downlink) and computational latency per client;
    \item \textbf{Operational Fitness:} The compliance of each configuration with practical edge deployment requirements.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Experimental Control}
Internal validity is maintained by holding all factors invariant except the specific generative route and privacy mechanism under study. Every configuration in the experiment matrix (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_configs}) is evaluated under identical conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Identical Data Partitioning:} A locked train/validation/test split partitioned strictly by capture group. The real test split is locked and never accessed during hyperparameter tuning (train: 88,500 rows; validation: 12,000 rows; test: 30,000 rows; class quotas strictly enforced);
    \item \textbf{Consistent Non-IID Skew:} Partitioning of training rows over $K = 5$ edge clients drawn from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration parameter $\alpha = 0.5$~\cite{hsu2019}, fixed deterministically by random seed;
    \item \textbf{Uniform Preprocessing:} A standardized pipeline ($\log(1+x)$ transformation of skewed numeric attributes, standard normal scaling, clipping at $\pm 5\sigma$, and one-hot encoding of categorical headers), producing exactly 60 input feature dimensions;
    \item \textbf{Standardized Downstream Classifiers:} A Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001} with 200 estimators and a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with hidden layers $[128, 64]$;
    \item \textbf{Multi-Seed Replication:} Identical seed triad $\{0, 1, 2\}$ governing weight initialization, client partitioning, and synthetic sampling;
    \item \textbf{Fixed Real Test Set:} All reported utility metrics are evaluated on the exact same held-out real test set.
\end{itemize}
Under this design, differences in downstream performance are directly attributable to the generative model and privacy mechanism. Extraneous sources of variance, such as hyperparameter optimization and capture group boundaries, are addressed in Section~\ref{subsec:limitations}.

\subsubsection{Evolution of the Design}
The study was originally conceptualized around a federated conditional variational autoencoder (CVAE)~\cite{sohn2015}. Following initial empirical evaluation, the framework transitioned to a marginal-based generator, \textit{FedDP-Marginal}, as its primary generative mechanism, while retaining the federated CVAE as a baseline. This architectural pivot is formally documented in the project deviation log (\texttt{docs/SPEC\_DEVIATIONS.md}, entries O1.9 and O2.3). Maintaining the CVAE configurations in the benchmark matrix ensures a rigorous, head-to-head comparison under identical federation topologies, differential privacy budgets, and secure aggregation environments.

\subsection{System Architecture and Generator Routes}\label{subsec:system_arch}

\subsubsection{System Roles}
As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:system_arch}, the PP-FedData architecture comprises three primary entities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Edge Gateways ($K = 5$ FL Clients):} Each gateway observes local MQTT traffic and maintains exclusive custody of its telemetry. Due to non-IID Dirichlet partitioning, individual gateways frequently exhibit severe data sparsity or complete absence of specific rare attack classes.
    \item \textbf{Aggregation Server:} The central coordinator orchestrates communication rounds, computes secure aggregates of client submissions, reconstructs global generative models, and generates synthetic records. The server is modelled as \textit{honest-but-curious} (Section~\ref{subsec:threat_model}).
    \item \textbf{Downstream IDS:} Classifiers (Random Forest and MLP) are trained on generated synthetic traffic (TSTR) or hybrid datasets (TAug, TAugR, local augmentation) and evaluated on the locked real test set.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Synthesis Locality and Server Observability}
Synthetic data records are synthesized centrally at the aggregation server, conditional on specified class labels, either by decoding latent vectors via the global CVAE decoder or by ancestral sampling over the reconstructed global dependency tree and marginal tables. Edge gateways do not generate synthetic traffic locally.

The visibility of client updates to the aggregation server is strictly determined by the active privacy configuration:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SecAgg$^+$ Protocols (M2, M3, MGs, MGr):} Utilizing Flower's SecAgg$^+$~\cite{bell2020, beutel2020}, the server observes only masked parameter vectors, reconstructing strictly the additive sum while learning zero information regarding individual client contributions;
    \item \textbf{Plain FedAvg (B3, M1):} The server observes raw individual client model updates. In M1, updates are perturbed locally via DP-SGD;
    \item \textbf{Local DP (MGl):} Gateways inject full calibrated noise into local tables prior to plaintext transmission, allowing the server to observe individual noisy tables without cryptographic masking;
    \item \textbf{Simulated Distributed DP (MGd, MGb):} The distributed summation is computed within a single execution process to benchmark accounting efficiency.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Comparison of the Two Generator Routes}
Table~\ref{tab:two_routes} contrasts the fundamental architectural mechanisms of the two routes. FedDP-Marginal exchanges discrete frequency counts across three synchronized releases, whereas the federated CVAE exchanges continuous parameter weight vectors over 30 iterative FedAvg rounds.

This operational divergence is decisive for differential privacy accounting. In FedDP-Marginal, each release represents a linear additive summation with bounded global sensitivity; calibrated noise can therefore be divided among clients and reconstituted exactly via secure aggregation. In contrast, DP-SGD~\cite{abadi2016} introduces non-linear gradient transformations across successive local optimization epochs, where noise added by one client interacts non-linearly with others; distributed DP is consequently mathematically invalid for the CVAE route (\texttt{SPEC\_DEVIATIONS.md}, entry O2.1).

For FedDP-Marginal, secure summation is implemented via a lightweight client-side patch: stock Flower SecAgg$^+$ transmits masked vectors as 64-bit integers. \subsection{Threat Model, Trust Assumptions, and Privacy Units}\label{subsec:threat_model}

\subsubsection{Adversary and Trust Assumptions}
The aggregation server is modeled as \textit{honest-but-curious} (semi-honest)~\cite{goldreich2004}: it faithfully executes protocol steps but inspects all incoming payloads to infer private telemetry. Gateways maintain physical control of their own data. We analyze four trust settings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Base Setting:} All $K$ gateways adhere to protocol and inject their assigned noise share ($1/K$ of the total required variance in distributed variants);
    \item \textbf{$h$ Honest Gateways:} The privacy guarantee is audited under scenarios where only $h \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ clients inject noise, while the remaining $K-h$ clients collude with the server or drop out. Effective $\varepsilon$ increases monotonically as $h$ decreases;
    \item \textbf{Threshold $t$:} Noise is calibrated such that target $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ holds whenever at least $t$ gateways contribute, with each client injecting $1/t$ of total variance (default $t = K$);
    \item \textbf{Local DP (MGl):} Each client adds the full required noise independently~\cite{kasiviswanathan2011}, achieving protection without trusting peer gateways.
\end{enumerate}
Malicious attacks, including data poisoning, model poisoning, and Byzantine corruption, are considered out of scope.

\subsubsection{Roles of SecAgg$^+$ and Differential Privacy}
Table~\ref{tab:protection_mechanisms} emphasizes that Secure Aggregation and Differential Privacy address orthogonal vulnerabilities and cannot be substituted for one another.

\subsubsection{Privacy Unit Granularity}
By default, $\varepsilon$ protects a single network packet record. For FedDP-Marginal, two higher-order privacy units are formalized:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{TCP Stream (\texttt{-strm\{m\}}):} Protects entire bidirectional TCP sessions;
    \item \textbf{Capture Group (\texttt{-cap\{m\}}):} Protects complete contiguous traffic captures.
\end{enumerate}
These guarantees hold strictly under two operational constraints: each unit is hosted entirely on a single client node, and each client clamps contributions to at most $m$ packets per unit. Under these conditions, releasing $T$ contingency tables incurs global sensitivities $\Delta_1 = m \cdot T$ in $\ell_1$ and $\Delta_2 = m\sqrt{T}$ in $\ell_2$ (\texttt{group\_dp.py}). In contrast, under packet-level DP, an attack session spanning $k$ packets is protected only via group privacy bounds, which scale weakly to approximately $k\varepsilon$~\cite{dwork2014}. The training dataset encompasses 76,463 TCP streams and 147 capture groups (ranging between 62 and 1,277 packets per capture).

\subsubsection{Public Side Information}
The following metadata is considered public and excluded from privacy budget accounting:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attack class label names and global sample quotas;
    \item Client sample counts $n_i$ when transmitted in cleartext (e.g., Flower's \texttt{num\_examples} in CVAE routes; held static in FedDP-Marginal);
    \item Preprocessing normalization parameters, logarithmic transformation choices, and categorical encodings computed centrally on pooled data prior to partitioning;
    \item Hyperparameter selections obtained from the validation split.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Requirement-Driven Recommendation Framework}\label{subsec:req_rule}

\subsubsection{Motivation and Relation to Heuristic R6}
Standard benchmark studies typically rely on single static selection heuristics. For instance, heuristic rule R6 selects the configuration maximizing Random Forest TAug Macro-F1 subject to membership inference AUC $\le 0.55$, worst-case $\varepsilon \le 5$, and per-round execution time $\le 3\times$ baseline B3. However, static rules cannot adapt to heterogeneous real-world deployments with varying bandwidth, mutual trust, or threat priorities. PP-FedData introduces a requirement-driven decision engine (\texttt{ppfeddata recommend}) operating over an 11-dimensional requirement tuple:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{R} = \langle & \text{Trust}_{\text{srv}}, \text{Trust}_{\text{cli}}, \varepsilon_{\max}, \text{BW}_{\text{rnd}}, \text{BW}_{\text{tot}}, \\
& \text{Rounds}_{\max}, \text{Unit}, \text{Priority}, \text{Recall}_{\text{rare}}^{\min}, \text{MIA}^{\max}, h \rangle
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Five-Stage Recommendation Procedure}
Operating over empirical scorecard metrics extracted directly from the immutable run ledger, the selection algorithm executes five successive filters:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Validity Filter:} Excludes configurations whose operational properties diverge from verified ledger execution;
    \item \textbf{Trust Filter:} If the server is untrusted, only configurations supporting SecAgg$^+$ or Local DP are retained. If client collusion is possible, distributed DP is audited at $h$ honest clients;
    \item \textbf{Constraint Pruning:} Eliminates configurations violating specified ceilings on $\varepsilon$, bandwidth, convergence rounds, or MIA leakage, or failing to satisfy rare-class recall floors;
    \item \textbf{Pareto Optimization:} Extracts non-dominated configurations across utility, privacy, and resource dimensions;
    \item \textbf{Tie-Breaking:} Selects the configuration maximizing the prioritized performance objective (Macro-F1, Binary F1, or Rare-Class Recall).
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Label Harmonization and Rare Classes}
Target labels are harmonized into a six-class configuration, namely \texttt{NORMAL}, \texttt{BCF}, \texttt{DELAYED}, \texttt{SYN}, \texttt{INVALID}, and \texttt{WILL}, in which the DoS and DDoS variants of the same attack family are merged into a single class. This merging is supported by leakage check C5 (Section~\ref{subsubsec:leakage_checks}), which shows that DoS and DDoS variants within the same family are practically indistinguishable at the packet level. The original 11-class labeling remains available in the data pipeline but is used only for the robustness analysis (Table~\ref{tab:robustness_branches}).

Rare classes are defined as attack classes whose training quota does not exceed 5,000 rows, i.e., \texttt{DELAYED}, \texttt{SYN}, \texttt{INVALID}, and \texttt{WILL}. Their rarity during training is a deliberate consequence of the quota design rather than a lack of raw data: as shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_composition}, the raw dataset contains between 270,529 and 601,542 packets for each of these classes. The quotas impose a 60:1 imbalance between the majority class (\texttt{NORMAL}, 60,000 rows) and the smallest minority class (\texttt{WILL}, 1,000 rows), which creates a demanding setting for evaluating synthetic data generators under federated constraints~\cite{he2009}. In contrast, the validation and test sets are class-balanced (2,000 and 5,000 rows per class, respectively), so that the evaluation metrics are not dominated by the majority class.

\subsubsection{Schema Harmonization and Feature Selection}
Preprocessing begins by harmonizing the heterogeneous raw packet exports. Column names are converted to snake case. Wireshark text labels (e.g., \texttt{message\_type}, \texttt{qos\_level\_*}, and \texttt{requested\_qos}) are normalized to canonical codes, and boolean indicators (True/False, Set/Not set) are mapped to 0/1. Redundant columns with the suffix \texttt{\_1} are removed. For multi-valued cells that contain several MQTT messages, only the first element is retained, consistent with the Normal export produced with the TShark \texttt{occurrence=f} option; the \texttt{n\_mqtt\_msgs} column is therefore discarded. Rows are then filtered to those whose protocol is TCP or MQTT, followed by a format audit of time-based tokens.

To prevent data leakage~\cite{kaufman2012, arp2022}, identifier and metadata columns are removed: \texttt{no} (row sequence), \texttt{epoch\_time} (absolute timestamp), and \texttt{time\_since\_reference\_\allowbreak or\_first\_frame} (which reveals the capture start time). The \texttt{stream\_index} column is retained only as grouping metadata and is not used as an input feature. The \texttt{source} column is removed because sender IP addresses would allow a model to memorize host identities instead of learning traffic patterns. Free-text payloads in the \texttt{info} column and the Normal-only \texttt{label} and \texttt{capture\_id} columns are also dropped; the raw dataset contains no MAC address or port columns. After feature selection, the final schema comprises $D = 60$ input features, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:feature_schema}.

\subsubsection{Group-Based Splitting and Quota Sampling}
The 11 fine-grained subclasses (\texttt{NORMAL} plus the DoS and DDoS variants of the five attack families) are partitioned with a group-based strategy: entire capture groups are assigned to the training, validation, or test split according to a fixed \texttt{split\_seed}. This prevents highly correlated packets from the same TCP stream or capture session from appearing in more than one split~\cite{arp2022}.

However, 8 of the 11 subclasses originate from a single capture file, which precludes a purely group-based assignment. For these subclasses, the validation and test sets are extracted as contiguous blocks of the same capture used for training (\textit{block split}), separated by 1,000-row buffers to mitigate adjacency leakage. TCP streams that cross a block boundary are discarded entirely to preserve stream integrity. This removal eliminates between 1.2\% and 9.9\% of the eligible rows within each affected subclass and introduces a slight sampling bias toward shorter connections.

After splitting, rows are drawn by random sampling without replacement according to the class quotas in Table~\ref{tab:quota}, which emulate the data scarcity faced by individual gateways. The training quotas enforce severe imbalance, whereas the validation and test quotas are balanced. The 30,000-row test set is locked and used only for final reporting; all hyperparameter tuning, design choices, and threshold calibration rely exclusively on the validation set.

\subsubsection{Preprocessing and Encoding}
The preprocessing pipeline is fitted on the training split only; the validation and test sets are transformed with the stored parameters to prevent leakage. Missing or non-applicable numeric values are imputed with 0, and a binary \texttt{\_is\_na} indicator is added for every feature whose missing rate in the training set lies strictly between 0.005 and 0.995.

Numeric features with a skewness greater than 2 are transformed with $\log(1+x)$; time-delta features are first multiplied by 1,000 to convert seconds into milliseconds. Numeric columns are then standardized to zero mean and unit variance and clipped at $\pm 5\sigma$ to limit the influence of extreme outliers. Categorical features are one-hot encoded, retaining the ten most frequent training tokens per column; the remaining valid values are mapped to an \texttt{OTHER} token and missing values to a \texttt{NONE} token.

Normalization statistics, categorical vocabularies, and imputation parameters are fitted centrally on the pooled training set. This constitutes a methodological limitation: it assumes a centralized view of the data prior to federation, and these statistics lie outside the differential privacy budget $\varepsilon$ (Section~\ref{subsec:threat_model}).

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols and Statistical Decision Rules}\label{subsec:evaluation}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocols}
The evaluation determines how well an IDS trained under different data regimes performs on authentic network traffic. Regardless of the training data, every protocol is evaluated on the same locked 30,000-row real test set (Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}), which provides a consistent benchmark across configurations. Building on the train-on-synthetic, test-on-real paradigm~\cite{esteban2017}, the following protocols are defined:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TRTR (Train Real, Test Real):} Upper-bound reference in which the IDS is trained on the pooled real training data, including variants with balanced class weights (B1a) and SMOTE oversampling~\cite{chawla2002} (B1b);
    \item \textbf{TSTR (Train Synthetic, Test Real):} Measures the standalone value of synthetic data when raw data cannot be pooled; the IDS is trained exclusively on synthetic records (5,000 rows per class);
    \item \textbf{TAug (Train Augmented, Test Real):} Assesses whether synthetic data adds value when real data is available; the real training data is augmented with synthetic records until each class contains 20,000 rows;
    \item \textbf{TAugR (Train Augmented Rare, Test Real):} Injects synthetic records only into the rare classes, at an augmentation ratio selected on the validation set;
    \item \textbf{Local Augmentation:} Simulates gateways improving their own IDS; each client supplements its real data with synthetic records from the global model only for the classes it lacks (at most 5,000 rows per class), yielding 15 client-specific models (5 clients $\times$ 3 seeds);
    \item \textbf{IDS-Side Options:} Examines whether the IDS can compensate for data limitations by using the full 20,000 synthetic rows per class together with class-probability weights tuned on the validation set;
    \item \textbf{Direct FedMLP:} Non-generative reference in which a classifier is trained directly with FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017}, optionally under DP and SecAgg$^+$, without an intermediate synthetic data step.
\end{itemize}
Two downstream classifiers are implemented with scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011}: a Random Forest~\cite{breiman2001} with 200 trees, and an MLP with two hidden layers of 128 and 64 units, early stopping, and at most 100 training iterations.

\subsubsection{Statistical Decision Rule}
To decide whether a configuration $A$ is genuinely superior to a baseline configuration $B$, a dual-condition decision rule is applied instead of comparing point estimates. Let $S$ denote the number of random seeds and $F_{A,s}$ the primary utility metric (macro-F1) of configuration $A$ at seed $s$. The mean paired difference across seeds is
\begin{equation}
\bar{\Delta} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\left(F_{A,s} - F_{B,s}\right).
\label{eq:mean_diff}
\end{equation}
Configuration $A$ is deemed to have a significant and practically relevant effect over $B$, denoted $A \succ B$, if and only if the 95\% confidence interval of the paired bootstrap mean difference excludes zero and the absolute mean difference exceeds the larger seed-to-seed standard deviation of the two configurations:
\begin{equation}
A \succ B \iff 0 \notin \mathrm{CI}_{95\%}(\bar{\Delta}) \land |\bar{\Delta}| > \max(\mathrm{sd}_A, \mathrm{sd}_B).
\label{eq:decision_rule}
\end{equation}
This study does not report $p$-values and applies no multiple-testing correction; all conclusions rely solely on rule~\eqref{eq:decision_rule}.

\subsubsection{Stream-Level Sensitivity Check}
Because packets belonging to the same TCP stream are not independent and identically distributed, the bootstrap procedure is repeated with resampling at the TCP stream level instead of the row level. Any configuration for which the stream-level bootstrap changes the outcome of rule~\eqref{eq:decision_rule} is explicitly flagged in the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsubsection{Environment Specifications}
All experiments were executed on an Intel x86-64 testbed with 16.9\,GB RAM running Windows 11 (Table~\ref{tab:env_spec}). Federated client nodes were simulated as isolated Ray actors sharing physical CPU cores.

\subsubsection{Reproducibility Protocol}
Experimental reproducibility is enforced via eight procedural safeguards:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Immutable Run Ledger:} Each execution appends cryptographic commit hashes and configuration digests to \texttt{results/runs.csv};
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Resumption:} Checkpointing prevents duplicate runs by validating pending configurations against the ledger;
    \item \textbf{Automated Artifact Generation:} All figures and tables are generated programmatically (\texttt{ppfeddata aggregate});
    \item \textbf{Locked Dependency Manifest:} Complete environment manifests and SHA-256 digests are preserved under \texttt{results/repro/};
    \item \textbf{Acceptance Verification:} Automated checks (\texttt{python -m ppfeddata.cli accept}) validate ten DoD criteria;
    \item \textbf{Clean-Copy Validation:} Phases 1--5 were re-executed from raw archives in an isolated environment;
    \item \textbf{Controlled Trial Auditing:} Seed-0 baseline runs were audited row-by-row against reference checkouts;
    \item \textbf{Cryptographic Data Guards:} Five automated leakage checks (C1--C5) verify feature arrays against stored cryptographic hashes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}\label{subsec:limitations}
The empirical conclusions of this study are bounded by the following methodological constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data and Granularity:} Evaluated on a single IoT dataset (DoS/DDoS-MQTT-IoT~\cite{alatram2023}) at the packet level. Inter-packet temporal correlations across extended sessions are not modeled. The Normal subset was re-exported from the original PCAP files and additionally contains broker-originated packets that the authors' export settings exclude (Section~\ref{subsubsec:provenance});
    \item \textbf{Privacy Guarantees:} Guarantees apply at the packet level by default. Stream-level guarantees assume strict participation ceilings. Normalization statistics and hyperparameter tuning are conducted centrally on validation data outside the DP budget;
    \item \textbf{Generative Expressiveness:} FedDP-Marginal relies on first-order tree factorizations; second-order structures (PrivBayes) represent an extension. The deep route evaluates a single CVAE topology;
    \item \textbf{Statistical Power:} Multi-client simulations utilize three random seeds $\{0, 1, 2\}$, which capture variance but do not permit high-order asymptotic claims;
    \item \textbf{Physical Simulation:} Network latency, packet dropouts, and hardware-constrained edge battery depletion are simulated within a shared CPU environment rather than evaluated on physical microcontrollers.
\end{itemize}
